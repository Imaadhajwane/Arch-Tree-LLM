What the study shows

Four claims survive the full pipeline: 12 seeds, per-family Holm correction, held-out seeds for the design choices, and comparisons at equal training cost rather than equal evaluation counts.

Beating evolution on accuracy

ArchTree ties regularized evolution and local search on accuracy. Its edge is cost: against RE under the same gate, 19 to 23% fewer GPU-hours at budget 200 (12 to 19% on held-out seeds).

TOST shows equivalence within ±0.5 points in only a minority of cells, so the paper says "no significant difference", not "equivalent".

One loop, seven decisions per proposal

Each iteration proposes one cell, refuses to pay for it twice, asks a free proxy whether it is worth training, and only then looks up its real accuracy. Select a step to see what it does, the setting used in every reported run, and the evidence for it.

The flaw the rank reward fixes: a reward that cannot tell branches apart

UCB adds an exploration bonus to each branch's value. With raw accuracy/100 as the value, every NAS-Bench-201 branch sits between about 0.85 and 0.95, while the exploration bonus at the old C = 1.414 is worth about 1 to 2.5, so the search just balanced visit counts. ArchTree instead scores a branch by the percentile rank of its accuracy among this run's evaluations.

When should a gate correct for cost? A closed-form answer

Zero-cost proxies favour big networks, which cost more to train. Penalising predicted cost sounds like the fix. In a Gaussian-copula model of accuracy A, proxy P and cost D, the gate score S = z(P) − λ·z(D) has

d corr(S, A) / dλ at λ = 0 = rPA · rPD − rDA

A small penalty helps only if this slope is positive. Try your own correlations on the left, or load a benchmark's measured values; the right panel shows what was predicted for each benchmark before the runs, and what the runs then showed.
